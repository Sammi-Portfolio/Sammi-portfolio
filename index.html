Where I want to go

Vertical operating systems are the interest. Frontier models keep getting better, which means most thin products built on top of them will get killed. The bigger opportunity is rebuilding industry software from the ground up for self-evolving, agentic systems. Many incumbent tools sit on rigid infrastructure and legacy workflows that are expensive and slow to transform, creating room for a new wave of disruptors. What survives will not win on raw intelligence; that gets commoditised. It will win by becoming the operating layer for one industry: deeply integrated, sticky, and designed so well that nothing general competes with it. Saturn is the shape: embedded in how financial advice firms actually operate, painful to remove. Granola is the craft standard.

The route I am interested in is owning one segment or industry deeply, the way Saturn is becoming embedded for financial advisers. Enter through a narrow, painful problem, then expand across the operating chain until the category runs on our layer. The moat is not the model. It is integration depth, product design, industry understanding and becoming the default system of record for innovation in that category.

I do not know the exact vertical yet. The route is likely a mix of transformation deployments inside real companies, a co-founder with deep industry insight, and direct research into where workflows are most broken. What I am not doing is picking one from a desk.

What is changingThe model itself gets cheaper. The value moves to distribution, industry depth and being properly embedded.
How I might enterTest scoped problems in real deployments, learn where the pain is sharpest, and let the strongest segment reveal itself through use.
Why I can do thisI can sit inside messy operating problems, understand the judgement calls, and turn them into something engineers can actually build.

My Story

My Story

Founder & commercial operator - shaped by risk, excellence and a need to build.

I grew up around two ideas that quietly shaped everything: risk was not something to avoid, and excellence was not optional. From my mum, I saw day-to-day courage and long-range thinking. She talked about Ethiopia's transformation years before it became obvious, then caught part of that tailwind herself. From my dad, I inherited a standard for excellence: a brilliant mathematician who earned a scholarship to study in the UK and built his path through civil engineering.

That combination made me believe I could do more than the normal path suggested. It also made it hard to apply myself to things that felt small, capped or disconnected from real impact. I wanted to solve difficult problems, build something that mattered, and create enough wealth to fund the people, dreams and ideas I care about.

I started building businesses at 17. Before turning 21, I had already started four. The first was a property deal-sourcing agency. I cold-called estate agents, spoke to investors and went to viewings as a teenager trying to create opportunity from nothing, but the motivation was wrong. I wanted money more than I wanted to master the problem. The people who win there have a real passion for crafting great deals and an unreasonable attention to detail. I did not have that, and I could not see myself spending a serious chunk of my life in that world.

TTM Capital came next: a Web3 project I built with a friend. I led growth, strategy and community, helped grow it to 1,800 members, raised £40,000, and sold out the launch. We later closed after spending too aggressively and overestimating demand. Much of the feedback had come from biased people, and we were gathering it in a way that reinforced what we already wanted to believe, which led us to overestimate the financial potential of the launch. That experience taught me to test demand more honestly and analyse feedback before building a forecast around it.

While studying, I worked in restaurants, hosting, sales and startups. A MarTech startup was where I really learned about AI, building production-ready agents that cut operational lag by 45%. Then came Badr Studios, where I delivered more than a dozen web, AI and digital transformation projects across fashion, technology, health, wellness and property.

Agency work taught me how to sell, scope and deliver quickly, but it also showed me how dependent AI is on good data. Around the same time, I read research on the demand for reliable financial data, which pulled me deeper into the space. I still felt the ceiling in client work though. It was useful commercially, but not the deeper mission I wanted to spend years building around.

ALFRD began differently. I initially wanted to build an operating system for fractional CFOs, but interview after interview exposed the same underlying problem: before finance teams could advise, forecast or use AI, their financial and operational data had to agree. That bottleneck became ALFRD.

I still approach building the same way. Recently, I walked around London carrying a board and pitching ALFRD to the public. I pitched to around eight investors I met that day and was interviewed on the spot by the Daily Mail about what I am building. It may look unconventional, but I am prepared to do what most founders will not.

I feel lucky to have been born into one of the most transformational periods in technology. I think solving problems gives people purpose, and it is one of life's beauties. I do not really see a version of life where I am not chasing problems, watching how technology changes society, and trying to use ground-up, first-principles thinking to transform industries at scale. I carry the forward-looking trait I saw in my mum, but now the tailwind is technology.

What actually drives me
  • I love risk because I grew up watching it create opportunity.
  • Excellence is the standard because my dad showed me what deep technical ability and discipline can unlock.
  • I need mission because capped work makes me restless, even when it is commercially useful.
  • I want leverage so the companies I build can create impact, wealth and freedom far beyond myself.
